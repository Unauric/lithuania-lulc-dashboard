Known limitations

  • All datasets are harmonised to 5 classes (Water, Wetland, Urban, Agriculture, Forest/Natural). Original classification nuance is lost.
  • Cross-dataset comparisons must account for large differences in spatial resolution (100 m – 25 km) and survey methodology.
  • Sub-basin zonal statistics are pre-computed; accuracy depends on raster–polygon alignment at each dataset's native resolution.
  • Raster files load on demand — first access per dataset/year may be slow depending on file size and connection.
  • Validation metrics (RMSE, MAE, R²) compare against CORINE CLC or GRPK cadastre snapshots; neither is a strict ground truth.
  • HYDE's "Natural (residual)" class covers all non-managed land, not only forest — direct comparison with "Forest" in other datasets is misleading.
  • LUCAS is a point survey; national shares are extrapolated and may differ substantially from raster-based datasets.

What the dashboard supports

The dashboard supports exploratory comparison of land-cover products: map + national or sub-basin shares over time, validation against CORINE or cadastre-based references, and exports. It is meant to complement deeper GIS or modelling work, not replace pixel-level accuracy assessment.

Basin-by-basin analysis

Each basin has a river running through it, surrounded by land — forest, farmland, towns, water, wetland. This checks whether a change in that land (say, more forest) tends to happen in the same years the river runs stronger or weaker.

How we check it: for every basin, we use one river gauge (near where the river leaves that basin) and yearly satellite maps of the land around it, from 2017 to 2025. Each year, we compare the two.

Here's a made-up example, "Example Forest", to show what the charts below actually mean:

Year by year, how far Forest cover and river flow each were from their own "normal" — 0 means a typical year, above 0 means more than usual, below 0 means less. Here, both lines rise and fall together: that's what a link between land and river looks like.

This one zooms into a single year (whichever year is showing on the map) and shows the same "above/below normal" idea for every land type at once, side by side. In this example year, both Forest and flow are above normal.

The two tests we run, Pearson and Spearman: Pearson checks for a straight-line link — as one thing goes up by a certain amount, does the other tend to go up (or down) by a matching amount, every time? Spearman checks something looser — does one going up generally mean the other goes up too, even if not by the same amount each time. We treat Spearman (shown as ρ, "rho") as the main number, since it doesn't get thrown off if a gauge's own readings bend the relationship a little (water level, for example, doesn't rise in a perfectly straight line with actual flow).

Example: say Forest cover went 10%, 12%, 15%, 30% over four years, while flow went 5, 6, 8, 9. Spearman says "perfect link" (ρ = +1), because both always moved up together, in the same order, year after year. Pearson would come out lower, because that last jump in Forest cover (15%→30%) isn't matched by an equally big jump in flow — Pearson cares about matching SIZE of change, Spearman only cares about matching DIRECTION and ORDER.

How much to trust a link — the q number: ρ close to +1 or −1 means a strong link; close to 0 means barely any link. q under 0.05 usually means it's probably a real link, not just luck — even after checking 5 land types at once (checking that many at once makes an accidental "hit" more likely by chance, so q corrects for that). Above 0.05, treat it as a maybe: most basins only have 6–9 years of data, and land cover and river flow are compared in the same year, so a link could just mean "a wet year," not "the land caused it."

Confidence range: when 2 or more basins are combined together (see "Combined" further down), you'll also see a range in brackets, like ρ = 0.65 [0.40, 0.85]. 0.65 is the best single guess; [0.40, 0.85] is how sure we are of that guess — based on the data we have, the real link strength is probably somewhere in that range. A narrow range (like [0.60, 0.70]) means we're fairly confident; a wide range (like [0.10, 0.95]) means we're really not sure yet, which is common with only a handful of years and gauges to go on.
